# ClientFlow AI Product Design

**Date:** 2026-11-01

**Status:** Proposed for approval

**Product:** ClientFlow AI
**Primary user:** Freelancers who need to turn vague client requests into clear, professional proposals

## Intent and Why

ClientFlow AI helps a freelancer transform an unclear client request into a reviewed, structured, shareable project proposal. A request such as “I need a website for my salon” becomes a guided discovery process, clear requirements, milestones, a timeline, and a price.

The product solves three related problems:

1. Clients often describe outcomes without supplying enough detail to estimate or scope the work.
2. Freelancers lose time deciding what to ask, organizing answers, and writing proposals.
3. Weak discovery creates unclear expectations, inaccurate estimates, and scope creep.

AI assists with analysis and drafting, but the freelancer remains responsible for every client-facing decision. Nothing generated by AI is shared automatically.

This project is also a learning vehicle and portfolio centerpiece. Development must expose the reasoning behind the design, backend, database, security, AI, testing, and deployment decisions rather than hiding them behind copied code.

## Product Promise

> Turn an unclear client request into a proposal-ready project without losing human judgment.

The shortest successful journey is:

```text
Unclear request
  -> AI-assisted discovery questions
  -> Client answers through a secure link
  -> Reviewed project requirements
  -> Editable proposal
  -> Client accepts, rejects, or requests changes
```

## Goals

- Let a freelancer create a client and save the client’s original request.
- Detect important missing information and generate relevant discovery questions.
- Let the freelancer edit, add, remove, reorder, and approve questions before sharing.
- Let a client answer through a secure link without creating an account.
- Turn the request and answers into editable project requirements.
- Produce an editable proposal containing scope, exclusions, deliverables, milestones, timeline, price, payment schedule, assumptions, and validity.
- Let the client accept, reject, or request changes through a secure proposal link.
- Preserve proposal versions and a useful activity history.
- Make the experience feel polished, trustworthy, warm, and distinct from generic CRM dashboards.
- Teach one coherent set of full-stack concepts during each development phase.

## Non-Goals for the First Complete Version

- Full CRM functionality such as pipelines, email campaigns, and sales automation.
- Project execution features such as kanban boards, time tracking, or team resource planning.
- Payment processing, bookkeeping, tax calculation, or real invoices.
- Legally binding electronic signatures or contract generation.
- Real-time chat between freelancer and client.
- Client accounts, client dashboards, or client password management.
- Multi-member agency workspaces and complex roles.
- AI autonomously sending messages, publishing requirements, or changing prices.

These may become later expansions only after the central request-to-proposal journey is excellent.

## Anti-Goals

The product fails even if technically functional if it:

- Becomes a collection of unrelated SaaS features.
- Makes AI feel more authoritative than the freelancer.
- Uses generic dashboard cards where a focused document workflow would be clearer.
- Forces a client to register before answering a form or reviewing a proposal.
- Hides important scope, price, or version changes.
- Looks impressive only with fake metrics, fake testimonials, or unrealistic demo data.
- Teaches installation commands without explaining requests, data flow, trust boundaries, and failure modes.

## Constraints and Decisions

- The existing project uses Next.js App Router, React, TypeScript, and Tailwind CSS.
- Freelancers authenticate with Clerk.
- Clients do not create accounts in the first version.
- Persistent data lives in Neon PostgreSQL and is accessed through Prisma.
- AI calls run only on the server; provider credentials never reach the browser.
- Public client pages use high-entropy, revocable, expiring access tokens stored safely rather than exposing database IDs.
- Every AI result is visibly marked as a draft and requires freelancer review.
- The application is responsive and keyboard accessible, with desktop as the primary authoring surface and mobile support for review and client forms.
- Development follows vertical slices: each milestone includes the relevant interface, server behavior, data model, validation, authorization, tests, and polish.

## Approaches Considered

### Chosen: Vertical slices

Build one complete part of the journey at a time. Each slice crosses the interface, backend, database, security, and tests and ends as working, demonstrable software.

This approach was chosen because it makes the learning sequence concrete, exposes integration problems early, and keeps the portfolio demo usable throughout development.

### Rejected: Technical layers first

Completing every screen before the backend would create a visually impressive shell quickly, but it would rely on fake data and delay learning how requests, authorization, persistence, and errors shape the interface.

### Rejected: Showcase-first prototype

Building one polished scripted demo would produce screenshots quickly, but replacing mocked behavior later would encourage architectural shortcuts and rework.

## Product Surfaces

### 1. Marketing website

A public landing page presents ClientFlow AI as a real SaaS product rather than as a portfolio case study.

Core sections:

- Hero statement: “Turn unclear client requests into proposal-ready projects.”
- A salon-request example that visually transforms from a vague sentence into structured work.
- A concise explanation of the request-to-proposal workflow.
- Product previews of discovery questions and the proposal experience.
- A clear explanation of human-supervised AI.
- Benefits centered on faster discovery, clearer scope, and fewer misunderstandings.
- One primary call to action: create the first proposal.
- Sign-in access for returning freelancers.

The landing page must not use fake testimonials, fake customer counts, or invented integrations. Pricing is omitted until a real business model is chosen.

### 2. Freelancer workspace

An authenticated application where freelancers create requests, review AI drafts, manage discovery, prepare proposals, and view decisions.

Primary areas:

- Projects list
- New project/request flow
- Project room with a persistent stage trail
- Discovery-question editor
- Client-answer review
- Requirements editor
- Proposal editor and preview
- Share and decision history
- Account/settings area

### 3. Client experience

A distraction-free public experience reached through a secure link.

It contains only what the client needs:

- Project and freelancer context
- Discovery form or proposal document
- Clear progress and validation
- Save/submit behavior appropriate to the link
- Proposal decision controls
- Confirmation and next-step messaging

It does not expose freelancer navigation, internal AI notes, other projects, or private metadata.

## Visual Direction

The selected direction is the first visual concept: a refined editorial project room.

### Character

- Calm, intelligent, warm, and premium
- More like a collaborative document studio than a generic CRM
- Professional enough for consultants and agencies, approachable enough for solo freelancers

### Visual system

- Warm ivory or soft off-white base surfaces
- Deep ink/navy typography
- Restrained cobalt for primary actions and progress
- Soft peach for contextual AI assistance and gentle emphasis
- Serif display type for important document headings
- Clean sans-serif type for controls, labels, and body copy
- Generous whitespace and strong reading rhythm
- Dividers, alignment, and typography before borders and shadows
- Limited corner rounding; no excessive pills, glass effects, or nested cards

### Interaction principles

- One obvious primary action per stage
- AI explanations stay adjacent to the material they influence
- Editable AI drafts look editable, not finalized
- Important transitions explain what will happen before the user commits
- Autosave state is calm and visible
- Empty, loading, error, expired-link, and already-submitted states receive designed treatment
- Motion supports orientation and progress; it never delays work

## Information Architecture and Project States

The freelancer-facing project room uses these stages:

```text
Draft
  -> Discovery questions
  -> Waiting for client answers
  -> Requirements review
  -> Proposal draft
  -> Proposal sent
  -> Accepted | Changes requested | Rejected
```

A project can move backward when the freelancer reopens discovery or creates a new proposal version. State changes must be explicit and recorded; they are not inferred solely from which screen was last opened.

### Revision and dependency rules

- Reopening discovery marks approved requirements and unsent proposal drafts as outdated.
- The freelancer is shown what changed and chooses when to regenerate or revise downstream drafts.
- Regeneration never overwrites freelancer edits silently.
- A sent proposal is an immutable historical version.
- An accepted proposal is never overwritten or changed retroactively.
- Work proposed after a sent or accepted version is stored as a new proposal version and requires a new client decision.
- Activity history preserves the relationship between the source request, submitted answers, approved requirements, proposal version, and client decision.

## Core User Journeys

### Freelancer creates a project

1. The freelancer signs in.
2. They create a client or select an existing one.
3. They save the original request and optional context.
4. ClientFlow creates a draft project and shows the original request unchanged.

### Freelancer prepares discovery

1. The server analyzes the request.
2. AI returns missing-information categories and suggested questions in structured data.
3. The freelancer edits, adds, removes, reorders, or regenerates questions.
4. The freelancer approves the final question set.
5. ClientFlow creates a revocable secure link.

### Client answers

1. The client opens the secure link without signing in.
2. The client sees the freelancer, project context, and approved questions.
3. Answers are validated and safely saved.
4. The client reviews and submits the response.
5. The freelancer sees that answers are ready.

### Freelancer reviews requirements

1. AI combines the untouched original request with the submitted answers.
2. It produces structured draft requirements and identifies unresolved assumptions.
3. The freelancer edits and approves requirements.
4. Approved requirements become the source for the proposal draft.

### Freelancer creates a proposal

1. ClientFlow drafts the project summary, scope, exclusions, deliverables, milestones, and suggested timeline.
2. The freelancer supplies or edits pricing and payment terms; AI never decides the final price.
3. The freelancer previews the exact client-facing document.
4. Sending creates an immutable proposal version and a secure review link.

### Client decides

1. The client opens the proposal link without an account.
2. They review the proposal and choose accept, reject, or request changes.
3. A change request requires a written explanation.
4. The decision is recorded with its proposal version and time.
5. The freelancer sees the outcome and can create a revised version when needed.

## AI Responsibilities and Guardrails

AI may:

- Identify missing information categories.
- Suggest discovery questions.
- Summarize answers.
- Draft requirements, assumptions, scope, exclusions, milestones, and timeline suggestions.
- Explain why a question or requirement was suggested.

AI may not:

- Share content with the client automatically.
- Choose or modify final prices.
- Treat uncertain details as confirmed facts.
- overwrite freelancer edits silently.
- Make legal, contractual, or payment guarantees.

AI outputs should use structured schemas, be validated on the server, retain their source context, and clearly separate confirmed facts from assumptions.

## Technical Shape

The first version remains one Next.js application:

```text
Browser
  -> Next.js pages and Server Components
  -> Server Actions or Route Handlers at explicit mutation/API boundaries
  -> Clerk authentication for freelancer routes
  -> Authorization checks against record ownership
  -> Prisma
  -> Neon PostgreSQL

Server-only AI workflow
  -> request + approved context
  -> AI provider
  -> schema validation
  -> editable draft stored in PostgreSQL

Public client link
  -> opaque token validation
  -> narrowly scoped project resource
  -> expiry/revocation/submission checks
```

Separate services, queues, and background workers are unnecessary initially. They should be introduced only when an actual long-running or asynchronous workload requires them.

## Learning and Delivery Roadmap

Each phase ends with a visible, testable result. Later phases reuse concepts from earlier phases rather than replacing them.

### Phase 0: Product foundation and engineering habits

**Build:** Confirm scope, user journey, visual direction, route map, terminology, initial data relationships, repository conventions, and a small testing strategy.

**Learn:** How to turn an idea into requirements; goals versus non-goals; user journeys; vertical slices; Git commits; environment variables; client/server trust boundaries; and why architecture starts with product behavior.

**Outcome:** An approved product design and a sequence of implementation plans small enough to execute and review.

### Phase 1: Brand system and public landing page

**Build:** Replace the starter screen with the editorial ClientFlow identity, responsive navigation, hero, salon transformation story, workflow explanation, human-supervised AI section, product previews, and calls to action.

**Learn:** App Router pages and layouts; React component composition; TypeScript props; Tailwind CSS; design tokens; responsive design; semantic HTML; accessibility; images and fonts; metadata; and the difference between reusable components and premature abstraction.

**Outcome:** A distinctive public website that explains the real product and establishes the design language used by the app.

### Phase 2: Application shell and freelancer authentication

**Build:** Add Clerk, sign-up/sign-in flows, protected application routes, the editorial workspace shell, navigation, user menu, and meaningful signed-out/unauthorized states.

**Learn:** Authentication versus authorization; cookies and sessions; middleware/request protection; Server and Client Components; route groups; layouts; environment secrets; and provider boundaries.

**Outcome:** A freelancer can create an account, sign in, and enter a protected but still mostly empty workspace.

### Phase 3: Database foundation

**Build:** Connect Neon, add Prisma, create the first schema and migration, and implement a single reusable server-only database client. Initial entities cover FreelancerProfile, Client, Project, ProjectRequest, and ActivityEvent.

**Learn:** Relational databases; tables, rows, primary and foreign keys; one-to-many relationships; constraints; indexes; migrations; connection pooling; ORM strengths and limitations; and development versus production data.

**Outcome:** Authenticated freelancers can own durable records, with ownership represented explicitly in the database.

### Phase 4: First complete vertical slice — save a client request

**Build:** Projects list, new-project form, client creation/selection, request validation, persistence, project detail page, empty/loading/error states, and ownership tests.

**Learn:** Forms; controlled versus server-managed input; Server Actions or Route Handlers; HTTP and mutations; schema validation; error handling; optimistic versus confirmed UI; CRUD; authorization queries; and test-first feature development.

**Outcome:** A freelancer can securely create and revisit a real client request.

### Phase 5: AI-assisted discovery questions

**Build:** Analyze the request, generate structured question suggestions, display missing-information categories, edit/reorder/delete/add questions, explain suggestions, regenerate intentionally, and approve the final set.

**Learn:** Server-only AI calls; prompts as application code; structured output schemas; runtime validation; hallucination boundaries; loading and retry behavior; rate limits; token/cost awareness; storing drafts; and preserving human edits.

**Outcome:** A vague request becomes an approved discovery questionnaire without allowing AI to contact the client.

### Phase 6: Secure no-account client discovery form

**Build:** Generate a secure share link, public discovery page, question-type rendering, autosave or safe draft behavior, final submission, expiration/revocation, duplicate-submission handling, and freelancer answer notification inside the app.

**Learn:** Capability links; cryptographically strong tokens; token hashing; public versus authenticated routes; least privilege; idempotency; validation of untrusted input; abuse protection; and accessible form UX.

**Outcome:** A client can answer approved questions from any device without registering or seeing private workspace data.

### Phase 7: Requirements generation and review

**Build:** Review submitted answers, generate structured requirements, distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions, expose unresolved details, edit/reorder requirements, and approve a requirements version.

**Learn:** Data transformation pipelines; provenance; versioning; normalized versus snapshot data; structured AI output; deterministic business rules around probabilistic output; and audit-friendly editing.

**Outcome:** Client answers become an accurate, freelancer-approved project definition.

### Phase 8: Proposal editor and pricing

**Build:** Generate a proposal draft from approved requirements, edit sections, define deliverables, milestones, timeline, exclusions, assumptions, price, payment schedule, validity date, and a client preview. Add autosave and clear draft/saved indicators.

**Learn:** Complex forms; nested relational data; decimal money storage; dates and time zones; document composition; derived totals; reusable domain components; autosave trade-offs; and validation across dependent fields.

**Outcome:** The freelancer can create a polished, accurate proposal while retaining control of pricing and commitments.

### Phase 9: Proposal sharing, decisions, and versions

**Build:** Create immutable sent versions, secure review links, proposal view optimized for reading, accept/reject/request-changes controls, comments for change requests, decision confirmation, revocation, and a version/activity timeline.

**Learn:** Immutable snapshots; state machines; concurrency; idempotent mutations; audit trails; secure public actions; transactional updates; and why a sent document must not change silently.

**Outcome:** ClientFlow completes its central promise from vague request to recorded proposal decision.

### Phase 10: Workspace dashboard and product cohesion

**Build:** Useful project list filters, stage indicators, next-action guidance, recent activity, search, thoughtful empty states, onboarding, responsive refinement, and consistent notifications.

**Learn:** Query design; pagination; URL search parameters; caching and revalidation; information hierarchy; product onboarding; component APIs; and performance-aware rendering.

**Outcome:** Multiple projects remain understandable without turning the product into a generic analytics dashboard.

### Phase 11: Quality, security, and accessibility hardening

**Build:** Unit tests for domain rules, integration tests for data/authorization, end-to-end tests for the golden path, accessibility checks, rate limiting, sanitized rendering, secure headers, expired-link tests, error boundaries, logging, and recovery behavior.

**Learn:** The testing pyramid; behavior-driven tests; mocking boundaries; OWASP-style threat thinking; XSS, CSRF, injection, broken access control; observability; accessibility standards; and graceful failure.

**Outcome:** The primary journey is dependable, secure, and usable beyond a happy-path demo.

### Phase 12: Deployment and production operations

**Build:** Production environments, Neon and Clerk production configuration, environment validation, migrations during deployment, Vercel deployment, preview environments, domain/metadata, analytics, error monitoring, backups, and a launch checklist.

**Learn:** CI/CD; build versus runtime configuration; secrets management; preview deployments; database migration safety; logs and monitoring; performance measurement; and production incident basics.

**Outcome:** A live product with a repeatable deployment process rather than a one-time demo.

### Phase 13: Portfolio presentation

**Build:** A strong README, architecture diagram, database diagram, annotated screenshots, demo data, short walkthrough video, problem/decision/trade-off case study, testing evidence, accessibility notes, and a roadmap for later features.

**Learn:** Technical communication; explaining trade-offs; documenting architecture; presenting impact without fake metrics; and showing engineering depth to recruiters or clients.

**Outcome:** ClientFlow AI demonstrates product thinking, UI craft, backend fundamentals, AI safety, testing, security, and deployment—not merely framework familiarity.

## Definition of the First Complete Version

The first complete version is ready when a new freelancer can:

1. Discover ClientFlow through the landing page.
2. Create an account and sign in.
3. Save the salon request as a real project.
4. Review and approve AI-generated questions.
5. Share a secure discovery link.
6. Receive client answers.
7. Review and approve generated requirements.
8. Build and price a proposal.
9. Share an immutable proposal version.
10. Receive and view the client’s recorded decision.

This path must work with realistic data, responsive layouts, designed failure states, authorization checks, and end-to-end test coverage.

## Later Expansion Ideas

Only after the central journey is complete:

- Reusable discovery and proposal templates
- PDF export
- Email delivery and reminders
- Payment deposits
- Contract or e-signature integrations
- Agency workspaces and team roles
- Client accounts
- Industry-specific question packs
- Proposal analytics
- Multilingual client forms

## Decisions Left to the Builder During Planning

- The exact AI provider and model, selected when the AI phase begins based on current structured-output quality, cost, and availability.
- The exact schema-validation and testing libraries, provided they support the stated behavior and work well with the installed Next.js version.
- Whether individual mutations use Server Actions or Route Handlers; the choice should be made per boundary and explained during implementation.
- The exact monitoring and analytics providers at deployment time.
